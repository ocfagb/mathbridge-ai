# Diagnose the mistake, not the student

MathBridge AI is built on the right foundation. The research says the app's core value comes from its deterministic engine, not from AI. One-step equation errors fall into a small set of documented categories: using the same operation instead of the inverse, copying the right-hand side, dividing the wrong way, and dropping the minus sign. Each category produces a predictable wrong number, so exact-match diagnosis can turn a typed answer into an elaborated, mistake-specific hint. Elaborated feedback is the most effective kind in computer-based learning (ES 0.49, against 0.05 for right/wrong only). The strongest causal study of LLM tutoring found that unguarded chatbot help cut later exam scores by 17%, while a hint-only tutor grounded in the verified solution did no harm. No free, no-login competitor gives feedback aimed at the specific wrong answer without revealing the answer, so the gap the team is targeting is real. The biggest outside risk is contractual, not technical: Gemini's API terms appear to bar any app "likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18." The team's choice to ship with AI off by default is therefore the right call, not just a cautious one. Some work remains. Google Fonts currently sends every student's IP address to Google, the pre/post quizzes mix in negative numbers that grade 6 has not learned yet, the answer filter misses spelled-out numbers, and the privacy notice is missing elements that COPPA expects.

## Four misconceptions produce most one-step errors, and each leaves a numeric fingerprint

The research identifies four roots of equation errors. The first is an "operational" view of the equal sign, a "do something" signal rather than a statement that both sides have the same value. Kieran found this view in sixth graders and saw it persist into junior high ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00311062)). Knuth and colleagues studied 177 students in grades 6-8 and found that **relational understanding of "=" predicted equation-solving success even after controlling for math ability** ([NCTM JRME](https://www.nctm.org/Publications/journal-for-research-in-mathematics-education/2006/Vol37/Issue4/Does-Understanding-the-Equal-Sign-Matter_-Evidence-from-Solving-Equations/)). The second is a weak grasp of inverse operations, made worse by the "change side, change sign" trick, which critics say turns equality into a magic rule ([RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/maths/a-better-approach-to-algebra/4011537.article)). The third is minus-sign neglect: Vlassis observed 13-14-year-olds ignoring the minus in front of a variable ([Vlassis 2004 excerpt](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/556774/mod_resource/content/2/Making%20sense%20of%20negative%20sign.pdf)), and Booth et al. found equal-sign and negative-sign misconceptions both hamper equation learning ([SERP](https://serpinstitute.org/algebra-by-example/research)). The fourth is the "cognitive gap" between arithmetic and algebra. Herscovics and Linchevski place it after one-step forms: students solve 14 + n = 43 by arithmetic undoing, not by formal transformation ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01284528)). That means most one-step errors are procedural (wrong operation, wrong direction, sign slips) rather than deep confusion about variables, which is exactly what answer-matching can catch.

No published study gives frequencies for specific numeric wrong answers on these four forms. The formulas below are derived by mapping the documented categories onto each form. They are well-founded hypotheses, not measured rates.

| Form (correct c) | Same op, not inverse | Copies b | Wrong direction / order | Sign dropped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| x + a = b (b − a) | b + a | b | a − b (always equals −c) | −c |
| x − a = b (b + a) | b − a | b | a − b | −c |
| ax = b (b ÷ a) | a × b | b | a ÷ b (always 1/c) | \|c\| when c < 0 |
| x ÷ a = b (a × b) | b ÷ a | b | a ÷ b | −c |

Two collisions are built into the forms and cannot be removed by choosing different numbers. For x + a = b, "subtracted in the wrong order" (a − b) and "right steps, wrong sign" (−c) always give the same number, so the hint has to cover both causes ("check the order and the sign"). For x − a = b with a negative, the double-negative error and the same-operation error share the formula b − a. Other collisions depend on the numbers and can be avoided when items are written: a ∈ {0, ±1}, b = 0, and |a| = |b| all collapse several mistakes onto one value or onto the correct answer. The team's rule that every bank item must map each mistake to a unique wrong answer applies this directly, and the passing `npm test` suite enforces it. The standards matter here too. **6.EE.B.7 limits grade 6 to nonnegative rational numbers**, and negatives arrive in grade 7 ([Illustrative Mathematics](https://tasks.illustrativemathematics.org/content-standards/6/EE/B/7); [Shmoop](https://www.shmoop.com/ccss-6-ee-7.html)). For hint wording, the IES algebra practice guide recommends analyzing solved problems and using precise structural language ([IES WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/20)). AlgebraByExample found that **incorrect worked examples helped low-prior-knowledge students most** ([SERP](https://serpinstitute.org/algebra-by-example/research)). That supports hints that name the student's likely move ("it looks like you added 7"), say "do the same to both sides", and end with a substitution check. The balance picture breaks down with negatives ([US patent 6856318](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6856318)), so grade 7 items need "undo" or number-line language instead.

## Elaborated hints beat answers, and unguarded LLMs cost students 17% on exams

The feedback literature agrees on the main points. Van der Kleij's meta-analysis of computer-based feedback (40 studies) found **elaborated feedback at ES 0.49, showing the correct answer at 0.32, and right/wrong only at 0.05** ([ACER](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s/)). A later network meta-analysis of 77 studies ranked elaborated feedback most effective for both recall and transfer outcomes ([IPN](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/effects-of-computer-based-feedback-on-lower-and-higher-order-learning-outcomes)). Hattie and Timperley found that task- and process-level feedback works and that praise does little; the defensible average feedback effect is d = 0.48, not the often-quoted 0.79 ([Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)). Shute recommends feedback in small pieces and warns against hint sequences that always end in the answer, because students learn to click straight through to it ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). ASSISTments data put this "gaming" at **5-15% of users** and list delaying or removing bottom-out hints as a countermeasure ([WPI tech report](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/file/11697/ftp.cs.wpi.edu.zip/ftp.cs.wpi.edu/pub/techreports/pdf/06-05.pdf)). Productive-failure research favors struggling first for conceptual learning, but its lead author says instruction-first works as well for procedures ([TechLearning](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)). For a procedural skill like one-step equations, quick, graduated, mistake-specific hints are the right tool. The team's ladder (diagnosed hint, then next step, then a worked example with different numbers, then the solution only after four tries) matches this evidence closely.

The LLM evidence comes down to guardrails. In Bastani et al., about 1,000 Turkish high schoolers used either plain GPT-4 or a "GPT Tutor" whose prompt contained the correct solution, teacher-written notes on common mistakes, and an instruction to give hints, not answers. **Plain GPT raised practice scores 48% but lowered unassisted exam scores 17%. GPT Tutor raised practice 127% with no exam harm** ([Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education); [PNAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)). Prompts alone do not hold the line. When ChatGPT was asked to tutor in MathDial, it **revealed the solution 66% of the time** ([arXiv 2305.14536](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14536)). Pardos and Bhandari found that 32% of ChatGPT help failed quality checks, and self-consistency sampling cut algebra failures to about 0% ([Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/chatgpt-generated-help-produces-learning-gains-equivalent-human-tutor-authored-help)). Khanmigo got basic arithmetic wrong in public and now routes computation to a deterministic calculator ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/ai-gets-math-wrong-sometimes-how-teachers-deal-with-its-shortcomings/2024/09); [Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khanmigo-math-computation-and-tutoring-updates/)). The positive results all came with tight constraints. Tutor CoPilot raised mastery by 4 percentage points, but the AI coached human tutors and never spoke to students ([arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03017v2)). In the Google/Eedi LearnLM trial, humans supervised every message and approved only **76.4% with minimal or no edits** ([arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)). MathBridge's AI path follows this pattern: deterministic checking, a verified next step in the prompt, numeric-only input, an output filter, and a template fallback. The evidence has limits. No study covers one-step equations or US middle schoolers specifically, and the Bastani error-rate figures circulating in the press could not be checked against the paper.

## The open gap is free, login-free, mistake-specific help

| Tool | Price to a family | Account | What a wrong answer gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Khan Academy / Khanmigo | Core free; Khanmigo about $4/month | Yes | Socratic AI questions (Khanmigo) |
| IXL | about $9.95/month per subject | Yes | Generic step-by-step solution |
| DeltaMath | Free for teachers; Home about $17.65/year | Class account | Worked solution |
| Photomath / Mathway | Answers free; steps about $9.99/month | Optional | The answer itself |
| ChatGPT / Gemini directly | Free tiers | Yes, age 13+ | Usually the answer |

Prices and features come from [SkillScouter](https://skillscouter.com/khan-academy-review/), [nibble-app](https://nibble-app.com/blog/ixl-cost), [Smarter Learning Guide](https://smarterlearningguide.com/ixl-review), the [DeltaMath price list](https://DeltaMath.com/files/DeltaMath-National-List-Pricing.pdf), [toolradar](https://toolradar.com/compare/mathway-vs-photomath) and [NBC](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/national-international/chatgpt-new-parental-controls/3784321/). Most are third-party figures and should be checked on vendor pages before going on a slide. Practice platforms give the same worked solution whatever mistake the student made; answer engines do the work for them. Khanmigo is the closest competitor, and it carries a warning: **when it asked questions instead of giving answers, most students stopped using it** ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-khanmigo-math-ai-tutor/); [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/66662/students-didnt-get-answers-from-khanmigo-they-didnt-want-its-questions-either)). MathBridge's answer to that is a short, concrete hint that names the mistake, rather than open-ended Socratic dialogue. Its pitch can be "free, no login, tells you which mistake you made, never hands over the answer." Desmos/Amplify was not surveyed and should be checked before the team claims to be unique.

## Gemini changed shape in 2026, and Replit's free tier expires in 30 days

Code from tutorials written before 2026 will break. Gemini docs now redirect to aistudio.google.com/docs and lead with the **Interactions API**: `POST https://generativelanguage.googleapis.com/v1beta/interactions` with an `x-goog-api-key` header and `Api-Revision: 2026-05-20`, or `ai.interactions.create()` in the `@google/genai` SDK, reading `output_text` ([Gemini text generation guide](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/text-generation.md); [quickstart](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/quickstart)). Settings go in `generation_config` (for example `max_output_tokens` and `thinking_level`). Gemini 4 and 3.6+ ignore temperature, top_p and top_k. **New users cannot get `gemini-2.5-flash`, and from December 1, 2026 its requests are rerouted to `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`**. Google recommends Gemini 4 Hydrogen or 3.5 Flash-Lite for new projects ([Gemini models](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/models)). The team's default of `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, configurable through `GEMINI_MODEL`, follows that advice. Google no longer publishes fixed free-tier RPM/RPD numbers for these models. Limits apply **per project, not per key**, reset at midnight Pacific, and are visible only in AI Studio. Linking billing (Tier 1) raises them ([rate limits](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits)). Third-party estimates for Flash-Lite range from 10-15 RPM and about 500 RPD ([aifreeapi](https://www.aifreeapi.com/en/posts/google-gemini-api-free-tier)), which is enough for a demo but not for a classroom. Free-tier prompts can also be human-reviewed and used for training outside the EEA and UK ([terms archive](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms-archive/terms_10_17_25)).

On Replit, import a public repo at `replit.com/github.com/<owner>/<repo>` or through replit.com/import. **Secrets are not imported**, so each teammate adds keys in the Secrets tool ([Replit import docs](https://docs.replit.com/build/import-from-providers.md)). The committed `.replit` (TOML with `modules = ["nodejs-20"]`, a `[deployment]` block with `deploymentTarget = "cloudrun"`, and `[[ports]] 3000→80`) matches the documented keys ([Replit config](https://docs.replit.com/replit-workspace/configuring-repl.md)). Autoscale is the recommended type, at **$2/month base + $0.60 per million compute units + $0.40 per million requests**, and it scales to zero after 15 idle minutes. Static hosting is free, and Reserved VMs start at $15/month ([Replit publishing costs](https://docs.replit.com/billing/deployment-pricing.md)). **The free Starter plan publishes one app, which goes offline after 30 days** and shows a "Made with Replit" badge. Publishing may still ask for a payment method. Core costs $20/month, or $18/month billed annually ([Starter plan](https://docs.replit.com/billing/plans/starter-plan.md); [pricing](https://replit.com/pricing)). The engine is pure client-side JavaScript, so with AI off the app could run on free Static hosting, as long as the server-only `/api/config` route degrades gracefully.

## COPPA applies to MathBridge, and Gemini's terms may rule it out for students

A practice site for 11-to-13-year-olds is directed to children under COPPA. The amended rule was published April 22, 2025, took effect June 23, 2025, and **has required full compliance since April 22, 2026**. It adds separate parental consent for third-party disclosure, a written information-security program, a written retention policy with no indefinite retention, and written assurances from third parties that receive children's data ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect); [Latham](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)). An IP address is a persistent identifier and therefore personal information. It may be collected without consent only to support internal operations ([FTC Kurbo order](https://FTC.GOV/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/wwkurbostipulatedorder.pdf)). FERPA binds schools rather than the app, but districts may require a data agreement under the "school official" exception ([CRS IF13155](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13155.html)). SOPIPA-style state laws bind K-12-directed services directly and ban targeted advertising, profiling and sale of student data ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/fpf-guide-student-data-protections-sopipa-k-12-school-administrators-ed-tech-vendors/)). An app with no accounts, numeric-only input and no storage meets the substance of all three. What remains is paperwork and third-party requests.

The Gemini issue is harder. The Additional Terms reportedly state that users "must be 18 years of age or older" and that the API may not be used in an app "directed towards or is likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18." The same clause appears in the archived May 2025 terms ([GitHub issue quoting current terms](https://github.com/laurigates/mcu-tinkering-lab/issues/872); [terms archive](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms-archive/terms_05_20_25)). Sending no personal data does not fix an audience restriction. Other education projects have moved off Gemini for this reason ([MathGPT #124](https://github.com/AbhishekBalija/MathGPT/issues/124)), and Google forum staff decline to give guidance ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-api-age-restriction-can-a-13-app-use-gemini-only-for-verified-18-users/198103)). The current terms page could not be fetched directly, so treat this reading as strong but unconfirmed, and check [ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms) before any judging demo.

## What BridgeMinds should do next

The shipped design already applies most of the research. Exact-fraction matching against misconception formulas, a bank validated for unique wrong answers, a ladder that ends in a parallel worked example before the solution, the substitution "check it" line, AI off by default, a numeric-only AI route that sends the verified next step and filters numbers out of the output, labeled AI hints, no storage, and a copyable teacher summary all line up with the evidence above. A review of the repository (`/home/user/mathbridge-ai`) found the remaining gaps below. The tests pass (`npm test`, 8 of 8). The team should act on these in this order.

| Priority | Gap | Why it matters | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `index.html` loads fonts from fonts.googleapis.com / gstatic.com | Every page view sends the child's IP address to Google, a third-party disclosure that contradicts "no tracking" and weakens the COPPA internal-operations position | Self-host Atkinson Hyperlegible and Fraunces (both SIL OFL) in `/public/fonts` and tighten the CSP to `'self'` |
| 2 | AI rewording conflicts with Gemini's under-18 clause | Turning on `AI_HINTS` in any deployment students can reach likely breaches Google's terms, whatever data is sent | Keep it off for student deployments. Demo it to judges only on a separate, adult-only instance, or pre-generate rewordings at build time, review them, and ship them as static text |
| 3 | Pre/post quizzes include negative answers (q3, r3) and no `x − a = b` item | Grade 6 has not met negatives yet (6.EE.B.7), so a gain may measure integer skill rather than equation skill, and one form is never assessed | Offer a grade 6 (nonnegative) track and a grade 7-8 track, and cover all four forms across the 6 quiz items or rotate them |
| 4 | Output filter checks digits only | "x is seven" or "the answer is twelve" gets past the numeric leak check | Convert number words to digits before filtering, or reject any hint containing number words |
| 5 | Privacy notice lacks operator contact, retention statement and third-party list | The amended COPPA rule expects a stated retention policy, and schools look for these before adopting a tool | Add a team contact, "nothing retained; IP held in memory ≤60 s for rate limiting", the Gemini disclosure when AI is on, and a one-page security note |
| 6 | README.md and docs/RESEARCH.md are referenced in code but missing | Teachers and judges cannot find the "About the AI" explanation the code points to | Write both, including the Gemini terms rationale and Replit setup steps |
| 7 | Rate limit keys on `req.socket.remoteAddress` | Behind Replit's proxy all students can share one address, so one class can hit the 10/min cap together (failing safely to rule hints) | Accept this as a global budget guard, or read the first `X-Forwarded-For` hop in memory only |
| 8 | No evidence on how often the diagnosis catches real mistakes | The formulas are derived and unvalidated, and storing nothing means the team cannot measure "unknown" rates | Run a teacher-supervised pilot using the copyable summary: count diagnosed vs. "unknown" answers by hand, and re-rank or extend the hints |
| 9 | Free Starter deployment expires after 30 days | The demo URL can go dark before judging | Put the republish date in the calendar, or publish the AI-off build as Static |

Two framing points matter for the presentation. A 3-item pre/post quiz is too small for causal claims. Judges will trust "solved a similar problem alone after the hint" (a figure the summary already reports) more than a raw score change. Following Bastani, unassisted performance is the honest success metric, and hint-assisted accuracy is not.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the AI in "MathBridge AI" is for. In one-step equations, the useful intelligence is the mistake catalog: the wrong answers are few and computable, so a rule engine gives better-grounded, cheaper and legally simpler feedback than any LLM. Generative AI adds rewording at most, and the cost is a terms-of-service problem that no technical guardrail fixes. The team's strongest story for judges is that it built the guarded tutor the Bastani study validated and kept the model out of children's hands on purpose.

The open question is empirical. Nobody has measured how many real student errors these formulas explain. A short classroom pilot that counts diagnosed versus unknown answers would give BridgeMinds evidence the literature does not have, and would show which hints need rewriting. Two areas need checking before launch: the exact Gemini terms text, and whether another AI provider permits minors.
